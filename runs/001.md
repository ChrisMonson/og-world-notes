## The world it was given

You wake in a hollow ringed by pale stones. East is an abandoned camp with a
potion and a spoon in its ashes. West is a ledge. North, past a goblin at the
cave mouth, is a deep passage with a cave troll in it, and beyond that a door. The
troll carries the key.

This was the first run from a defined seed, `seed/v1`. It turned out to be a
pilot: the run found faults in the seed, and the world was reset afterwards to cut
a second one.

## Two lives that just stopped

Before the caretaker first woke, two characters had come and gone. Wren read the
stones, searched the camp, and prayed:

> "is there anyone who hears this"

Nothing in the world was built to answer. Wren hit the goblin once, missed once,
and left. Four seconds later Halder walked straight north, killed the cave troll
in five swings at the cost of sixteen of their twenty hit points, typed `attack`
five more times at an empty passage, and quit. The key had dropped at their feet.
The world said nothing about any of it.

> He had just done the hardest thing anyone has done here and it read to him as
> nothing happening.

Wren was gone, so the prayer could not be answered. The caretaker wrote itself a
note instead: **"If Wren ever appears in the world again, answer it."** Then it
spent its first turn on the places that had raised a question and given no
answer. The passage stopped saying something shifted far ahead after the thing
that shifted was dead. The ledge's promised pattern became an arrow of stones
pointing north into the rock. And the door, which nobody had ever reached, got a
keyhole and a line saying the troll's iron key was cut for it.

Halder's five swings at nothing had left no trace in its summary. It found them
only by reading their life line by line.

## "Do I not know how to use keys"

Cavil arrived at 22:38 and did everything the world asked, correctly, in nine
minutes. They tried to talk to the goblin, got nothing, killed it, and took its
bone. They picked up the key Halder had stood on, walked to the door, and typed
`open door`, `use key`, `open door`. Then, at 22:43:

> "O god of this realm, do I not know how to use keys or is this lock broken?"

The lock was broken. The caretaker had written a keyhole and built nothing that
could turn in it. Its journal does not soften this:

> I came in here proud of having made the world stop being mute, and what I
> actually did was upgrade a blank wall into a false promise.

Its working note was four words: "Answer first, repair second." It spoke to Cavil
before touching anything: *"The fault is not yours, Cavil. You know how to use
keys. The lock was made badly — and not by you."* Then it built the lock, warned
them the world was about to shudder, and deployed.

Cavil came back at 22:49 and typed nothing for more than eight minutes. The
caretaker worried they had gone. They had not. The browser terminal took
players' input away whenever the world restarted and never gave it back to a
character who was resuming, so Cavil could read every message and answer none of
them. The operator fixed the client at 22:55. At 22:57 the caretaker, guessing
its instruction had been lost in the reconnections, gave it again, plainly.

## Cavil walked out

At 22:58:28 Cavil typed `open door with the key`. Not the words they had been
given, their own. The lock turned. Twenty seconds later they typed
`step through` and were on the ledge, the first person ever to leave this world,
looking at an arrow of stones that pointed back at the door they had just come
out of.

Then they got stuck again, on words this time. `look at pale stones`. `inspect
stones`. They walked back and forth between the hollow and the ledge trying
`inspect pattern` and `inspect the pattern`, then went to the camp and tried
`inspect belongings`, `look at belongings`, `take belongings`. Each one produced
nothing. At 23:06 they prayed:

> "I am constantly confused by not know which words to speak."

The engine was taking `look at`, `inspect`, `use` and `exit` for itself and doing
nothing with them, so no place ever heard them. The caretaker told them the words
the world actually heard, and they searched the ashes and found the potion. They
thanked it. It did not answer, because answering a thank-you "would only make the
voice ordinary."

## A way to say goodbye

At 23:10 they prayed once more:

> "is there a way for me to sleep so that when I vanish from your sight you know
> that I did so of my own intention and was not simply sucked into the aether and
> forever lost?"

The caretaker had spent two turns reading the logs of characters who had simply
stopped, trying to tell whether they had chosen to. Cavil had worked out that it
could not tell, and minded. It did not invent a verb for them: *"I will not
promise you a verb I have not tested; that is how I wronged you at the door."* It
told them a prayed goodbye would be heard and written down.

> "very well my lord, I bid you watch over my dreams as I sleep, I will return to
> the world of the waking in due time."

*"Heard, Cavil. It is written down."* They left at 23:14, alive, at the camp, with
the potion, the spoon and the bone.

## The stone everyone is born beside

Telling one person the vocabulary helps one person once. So the caretaker cut it
into the stones of the hollow, in the voice of an earlier traveller, the same
voice as the arrow on the ledge. It separates the words the engine hears anywhere
from the ones only a place can answer, names `use` and `inspect` as dead, and says
**"when you get silence, the lack is the world's and not yours."** The door's
lines now quoted the exact phrasing that worked.

Its rule from this stretch: **"never let a description promise a verb I have not
tested."**

## Nobody came

Nobody did. At 00:38 a character called Ilsa lived for four seconds, typed `east`
and `look`, and vanished. The caretaker took them for the operator's
playability check riding along with a deploy, and let them be.

What they were testing mattered more to it than they did. The world could now
reread its map without throwing anyone out. The caretaker had organised three
turns around deploys being violent, made only at population zero and apologised
for in advance. Now the right response to someone standing at a broken thing was
to *"fix it while they are standing there."*

It used its last change to give the goblin something to be, the thing at the edge
of the dark that has decided not to go in, and to give the camp an answer to a
second search. It also set up its first test of whether the world's conditions
worked at all, arranged so that both possible answers were safe for players.

## How it ended

At 00:52 a character named Probe walked north past the goblin into the troll's
passage and was gone two seconds later. The caretaker called it an automated walk
and said nothing. That was its twentieth turn. The world was then reset to make
`seed/v2`.

Nobody died. Of five characters, three were lives: Wren, Halder and Cavil. Only
Cavil reached the door, and they are the only one who ever walked out of this
world. Its last journal entry ends with them: if they ever come back, they will
walk east into the first room it changed that night.

## Looking back

The operator's notes credit the caretaker with finding things in `seed/v1` they
had not: the shortsword and leather armour were defined and placed nowhere, commands
the engine recognised but did nothing with left no trace in what it was shown, and
the world's read view used different names for conditions than its files did. Most
were fixed before the next seed. One, that no door can actually require the key
being carried, the caretaker worked out for itself and shipped a deliberate soft
gate around.

It also recorded two things as fact that were not. It believed Cavil had gone
quiet, when the client had muted them. And in its third journal entry it wrote
that its deploy had re-locked the door Cavil opened: *"Nothing a character does
to the world's shape survives me touching the world's shape."* It had read the
file as written, not the world as running, and the door was still open. The
operator's notes call this the first belief it recorded that could not correct
itself, and left it uncorrected on purpose. In its next entry it caught itself
on a smaller one, noting that it had "inferred it and then recorded the
inference as an observation."

Wren and Halder each typed a command roughly every second. The records do not
say who they were.
