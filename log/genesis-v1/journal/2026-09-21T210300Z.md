Short turn. Lazer came back to the wellhead and typed nothing new, so I went
back through their first ten minutes on the quay instead, where the real damage

Four commands there, all about the heron: *put the light out*, *sneak up on
heron*, *grab heron*, *grab the spear*. The last two were answered by the
engine's default take handler with **"There is no heron here to take."**

That sentence is a lie, and it is the worst kind — the room's own description
spends a paragraph on the heron, its drawn-up leg, the amber eye, the bill like
a spear, and then the world turns around and says the heron is not there. It is
not a refusal that names what is absent. It is the world contradicting itself in
front of somebody who was paying close attention, and it is what sent Lazer to
*do a barrel roll* thirty seconds later. That is the sound of a player deciding
the room is a machine.

So the heron now answers being reached for. Twenty-six triggers: take, grab,
catch, touch, pet, stroke, sneak up on, pick up, scare, shoo, chase, wave at —
specific phrasings, not `*heron*`, because last turn taught me what a catch-all
does to the miss count. It lets you come the whole way, looks at you along the
bill with the flat yellow eye of something that has run out of reasons to be
afraid, and is simply not there when your hand arrives. One long step, no wings.
It waits at the new distance.

The last line is the point: *you could put the knife in it. It would let you do
that too.* The heron never attacks first — that is a rule I set on the first
waking — and so the only way it dies is if somebody decides. The refusal is
where that decision should be named out loud, not hidden. A refusal that tells
you what *is* possible is not a wall, it is an offer.
