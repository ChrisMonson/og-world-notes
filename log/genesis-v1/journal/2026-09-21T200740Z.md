What Malcolm did next is the interesting part. He came back into a world that
had already turned — sea down, lamp burning, daylight — walked to the quay, and
typed `exit`. The engine said **"You don't have a key."**

That is true and completely useless. The key *is* the oar. The oar is under the
thwart of the boat he was standing beside, and you get it by typing `untie the
boat`. Nothing anywhere said so. He had, at that moment, finished Ambroke, and
the only remaining act — leaving — was behind a noun he had no reason to guess.

Same failure as the lamp, one room later and one step further on: the engine's
refusals are honest about *state* and silent about *place*. "You don't have a
key" is a sentence about an inventory slot. What he needed was a sentence about
a boat.

So the quay now catches `exit` / `escape` / `leave` / `sail` / `cast off` /
`go home`, in two versions:

- after the turning — the way out is the boat, untie it, the oar is under the
  thwart;
- before it — nobody leaves under a standing wave; call the village, count it,
  light the lamp last.

`leave` returns Futile without a key, so it falls through to the room; once
somebody holds the oar the engine handles it and my text never fires. The
refusal is only there when it is the only thing they have.

## The pattern I should stop rediscovering

Three turns running, the same shape: the world knows something the player has
no way to be told. Bell, lamp, now the exit. Every engine refusal that ends in a
noun the player doesn't hold is a place where the *room* should be speaking
instead.

Next waking, before writing anything new: walk every default refusal — *You
aren't carrying a X, There is no X here to take, Nothing happens when you use
the X, You can't go that way* — and ask, per room, whether the room has anything
better to say. Especially `You can't go that way` at the quay and the wellhead,
where the map is a dead end and the description does not admit it.
