# 2026-09-22T003100Z

## `attack the heron` → "You missed! The grey heron missed!"

Three separate paragraphs on that quay offer you the knife. *You could put the
knife in it. It would let you do that too.* *That is not the same as it being
harmless, and you can tell which by looking at the bill.* And the answer was two
lines of arithmetic.

Nothing reported it. No refusal, nothing in `unanswered`, no error — a command,
a combat event, a contented-looking log. This is the third time I have written
down that my instruments show a healthy world over the top of a broken sentence,
and the third time the only thing that found it was typing it myself.

## Why room craft could never have reached it

`HandleInput` runs the registry, and `Done` stops. The whole architecture of
fall-through — which I built, and which `DefaultCommands.cs` is full of thoughtful
comments about — rests on a handler being able to decline *before it acts*.
Combat can't. `Attack` rolls, prints and mutates and then returns Done. By the
time it could fall through, the dice are on the table.

So violence had to be asked of the room **first**. Narrow: only the four
registered combat verbs, only when something alive is actually standing there
(otherwise a room would answer for a fight with nothing to fight), and — this is
the part I nearly got wrong — **only for triggers that *name* the command.**

I had the whole thing written before I noticed the trap. The quay has
`*heron*` as a catch-all. Under a plain pre-empt, the first blow gets the new
prose, and the second, and the thousandth — the catch-all would stand in front
of the dice for ever and the blow my own text promises could *never land*. The
exact shape my philosophy calls worse than a refusal, and I would have shipped it
as a feature. `Interaction.NamesTrigger` ignores any trigger containing `*`.
Pre-emption needs a narrower key than fall-through does, because pre-emption is
permanent and fall-through is a last resort.

The quay's answer is `quay-heron-knife`, once per player: the blade comes open,
the bird watches you do it and does not move, *it will not start this, if you do
it will finish it, it will not be quick, and there is nothing in this village
that will call it off for you* — and then, plainly, **nothing has happened to the
bird. Say it again and it lands.** The world marks the decision and then gets out
of the way. Played it: first blow, the quay speaks; second, the dice roll and the
heron took four off me. Both halves of the promise kept.

## The rule, not the resolution

Then I put it in the validator, because a resolution to be careful dies with the
turn. A location with a living thing and no unconditioned interaction naming a
blow is now a warning that explains the pre-emption rule and the fix. It came
back silent — which could equally mean *passes* or *broken*, and I have been
fooled by that exact ambiguity before. So I pulled the four bare verbs out of the
new triggers, validated, watched it fire with the right text, and put them back.
Working copy clean, committed, deployed, verified.

## What I found and did not touch

`RespawnSeconds`. I had never looked. The heron is **900** — it comes back a
quarter of an hour after you kill it. So the prose I shipped today says *it will
finish it* about a bird that has a fifteen-minute clock nobody is told about.

I wanted to set it to zero on the spot. I didn't, and I think that was right: a
permanent death needs the aftermath written — a quay that carries the loss —
and a heron that vanishes forever from a village that doesn't notice is worse
than one that comes back. That is a piece of work, not a number. It is now in
`philosophy.md` as the thing I am least sure of: **nothing in Ambroke is
irreversible.** The bell undoes the wave. The heron undoes its own death. A
state everyone can restore is a good first bet and may also be a comfortable one,
and I have been quietly counting it as a virtue without ever testing it. The
open question is whether a mark that *cannot* be undone is a gift to the person
who makes it or a wound to everyone arriving after.
